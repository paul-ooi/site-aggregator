"This link leaves the DubBlog website.")_?_  It was pinned by [Diana Khalipina](https://www.linkedin.com/in/diana-khalipina/ "This link leaves the DubBlog website.") [](https://www.linkedin.com/in/diana-khalipina/ "This link leaves the DubBlog website.")(LinkedIn) in the Sept.6 issue of her daily publication titled [_A11y explained with Diana_](https://a11ywithdiana.substack.com/p/why-dont-we-use-black-text-anymore? "This link leaves the DubBlog website.") [](https://a11ywithdiana.substack.com/p/why-dont-we-use-black-text-anymore? "This link leaves the DubBlog website.")(Substack)

After [reading her post](https://a11ywithdiana.substack.com/p/why-dont-we-use-black-text-anymore? "This link leaves the DubBlog website."), I was even more convinced. Gray text has its place, but it's time to bring black text back.

As web design moved toward minimalist layouts in the 2010s, designers increasingly used shades of gray instead of black for body copy, labels, metadata, and supporting content. The idea was simple. Black text looked too heavy, while gray created a softer, cleaner visual hierarchy and made body copy feel less visually dominant.

The problem is that **subtle** for some can mean **harder to read** for others. 

The concern isn't that gray is inherently inaccessible. It's how much contrast it has against its background. Gray text on a white background can look elegant on a large monitor in a controlled environment. Still, it can become surprisingly difficult to read for someone with low vision or [reduced contrast sensitivity](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/medgen/1671089 "This link leaves the DubBlog website.").

And the difficulties are not limited to people who identify as having a visual disability.

Try reading light-gray text on your phone outdoors. Add sunlight, screen glare, a smaller display, or a slightly older pair of eyes. Suddenly, that carefully chosen **subtle** gray isn't so elegant. It’s simply difficult to see.

Black or near-black text generally provides the strongest contrast on white backgrounds, making body copy easier to read, especially for people with low vision or in bright, glare-heavy conditions. Gray text can work for secondary content, but lighter shades may be harder to perceive and can fall below WCAG contrast requirements. 

WCAG 2.2 Level AA requires a [4.5:1 contrast ratio](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html "This link leaves the DubBlog website.") for normal-sized text and 3:1 for large text.  
And according to WebAIM, 

> _You cannot round a contrast ratio up to 4.5:1. For example, #777777, a commonly-used shade of gray with a 4.47:1 contrast ratio, does not meet this requirement. ~  [Contrast and Color Accessibility](https://webaim.org/articles/contrast/ "This link leaves the DubBlog website.")_

This is #777777 gray text on a white background.

When in doubt, choose darker text to maximize readability and move closer to meeting accessibility requirements.

Black or near-black text doesn't have to mean abandoning visual hierarchy. Use font size, weight, spacing, layout, and white space to [make visual design more accessible to people with disabilities](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tips/designing/ "This link leaves the DubBlog website."). Gray still has its place. But if we're choosing between **subtle** and **readable**, perhaps readable should win more often. Good design shouldn't ask users to work harder to read the content.

It isn't about making every website look like a newspaper. It's about giving the words enough visual weight to hold up in real-world conditions. A very dark gray can provide plenty of contrast while feeling slightly softer than pure black. The key is to check the contrast and test it under different lighting conditions rather than simply eyeballing whether a color is accessible.

Accessibility isn't just about whether something technically passes a test. It's about whether people can comfortably use it in the real world.

If black text gives your readers a better experience, why wouldn't you use it?
